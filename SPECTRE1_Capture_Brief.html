Bottom line

Bid a hybrid flash + CMR-HDD parallel file system. Not all-flash, not all-HDD.

The competition will not be decided on bandwidth. It will be decided on four physical and regulatory constraints that most OEMs' standard 2027 configurations violate on day one, and on a set of non-technical gates that eliminate vendors regardless of how good the array is.

The four constraints that decide this

3.1  No HAMR, no MAMR, no SMR — this caps HDDs at 26 TB

§3.2

"When applicable, use PMR/CMR technology for all HDDs; HAMR, MAMR, and Shingled recording devices are not permitted."

This is the highest-impact line in the document and it is easy to miss.

  • Seagate's 30 TB+ Mozaic drives are HAMR — excluded.
  • WD's 32 TB / 40 TB-class drives are UltraSMR — excluded.
  • Toshiba's high-capacity parts are MAMR — excluded.
  • The largest compliant drive is the WD Ultrastar DC HC590 at 26 TB (ePMR/CMR, 11-platter, SAS or SATA, 2.5M-hr MTBF), with 24 TB as the safe volume part.

Every OEM's default 2027 capacity-optimized quote will assume 30–40 TB drives. Under this rule they lose roughly 35–50% of the capacity per rack-unit and per watt they were counting on. Any competitor quoting HAMR or SMR is non-compliant; any who requotes on CMR watches their density collapse.

Open — resolve before sizing anything

Confirm a FIPS-certified SED SKU exists at 26 TB. The HC590 is documented with Instant Secure Erase, but a FIPS 140-2-certified or 140-3-compliant SED variant at top capacity is not confirmed, and the SED/FIPS SKU historically trails the leading capacity by a generation. If it does not exist, the compliant ceiling drops to 24 TB or lower and every capacity number in this brief moves with it.

3.2  Power, not floor space, is the binding constraint

§7.1

The electrical spec quietly sets the whole system envelope.

  • 3-phase 60 A / 208 VAC receptacles → 21.6 kVA, derated 80% continuous = 17.3 kW usable per feed.
  • Dual-fed diverse — one utility, one UPS. You must survive on one side, so design to 17.3 kW per rack.
  • Total cap 275 kW across production, cluster management, and the 5300-DC TDS.
  • 275 kW ÷ 16 production racks ≈ 17.2 kW/rack. The two numbers are the same constraint stated twice — ORNL sized the budget at exactly one 60 A feed per rack.

Cooling is not the limit: an RDHX at 11 gpm with a 15 °F delta removes roughly 24 kW, comfortably above the 17.3 kW electrical ceiling. Design to watts, not to BTUs or to rack units.

And because §3.2 requires the base proposal to occupy only 50% of production floor space = 8 racks, the base bid has a hard budget of roughly 138 kW.

3.3  What 138 kW actually buys

A WD Ultrastar Data102 4U102 JBOD draws ~1,300 W typical (1,600–1,800 W max). Working the numbers against the 8-rack base bid:

Media strategyMax usable in 8 racks / 138 kW400G endpoints affordableVerdict
All-HDD (26 TB CMR)~184 PB~20Capacity ✓ · bandwidth-starved ✗ · small-file ✗✗
All-flash (61.44 TB QLC)~250 PB~48–64Best fit for the envelope — but cost and NAND lead time ✗✗
Hybrid recommend~150–180 PB~30–40Balanced; lands at the low end of the required band
Two conclusions worth putting in front of ORNL

1. All-flash fits the facility envelope better than all-HDD. Spinning disk is what blows the power budget — 87 JBODs consume 114 kW and leave almost nothing for the servers carrying the 400G endpoints. This is counterintuitive, and it is the strongest argument for a flash-heavy hybrid.

2. In 8 racks you land near 150 PB — the bottom of the 150–250 PB band. Reaching 250 PB requires either the full 16 racks or an all-flash build. See Q1 in §06.

3.4  The file distribution is a metadata problem, not a capacity problem

§4.1

Converting the file-count distribution to bytes using log-midpoint estimates:

Bucket% of files≈ % of bytes
≤ 4 KiB40.2%~0.003%
≤ 32 KiB24.0%~0.016%
≤ 64 KiB8.1%~0.014%
≤ 256 KiB9.7%~0.06%
≤ 1 MiB3.8%~0.09%
≤ 4 MiB9.8%~0.9%
≤ 16 MiB1.1%~0.4%
≤ 1 GiB2.9%~21.7%
> 1 GiB0.5%~76.8%

72.3% of files consume ~0.03% of the bytes. Mean file size lands around 25–30 MiB, which at 200 PB implies roughly 4–10 billion inodes. The estimate is sensitive to the ≤1 GiB bucket midpoint — plan for 10 billion.

Core technical win theme

A flash tier of only ~0.5–1 PB usable holds every inode plus every file ≤ 64 KiB — absorbing 72% of all file operations — while HDD carries 99.97% of the bytes. Small files are cheap to solve here. Spend the flash budget on metadata and the small-file population, and the rest on capacity.

This is also where the file systems genuinely differ:

  • IBM Storage Scale (GPFS) stores file data inside the 4 KiB inode when it fits (~3.5 KiB payload). The 40.2% of files that are ≤4 KiB require zero data-block I/O — the read or write completes in the metadata operation itself.
  • Lustre needs Data-on-MDT plus PFL and DNE2 striping, with flash MDTs, to reach equivalent behavior. It works, and DDN and HPE both know how to tune it, but it is configuration rather than architecture.
  • VDURA/PanFS routes small files to the VeLO director nodes on NVMe by design.
  • WEKA distributes metadata across the cluster and is the strongest performer of the group on pure small-file IOPS.

Priced separately, per the RFP

  • On-site parts cache sized from MTBF to sustain two weeks with no refresh, including hot spare nodes of every type.
  • 1 × on-site System Software Analyst, DOE Q-cleared, four years plus 5th/6th-year options.
  • RHEL licensing and support, file system licensing, HA licensing.
Design lever most bidders miss

Prefer small SSU granularity, and minimize the number of distinct node types. Two TDS systems each requiring "a minimal set of each component" is a fixed cost that scales with how coarse the building block is — and §5.2 separately requires hot spare nodes of each type in the parts cache. Every additional node type is paid for twice. A vendor whose minimum viable unit is a 2U flash + 4U disk pair prices both line items far better than one whose minimum unit is a populated rack.

Clarification questions to submit

Ordered by how much the answer changes the bid.

  1. Does the 150–250 PB usable requirement apply to the 8-rack base proposal, or to the full 16-rack build-out? Under the strict reading the answer is ~150 PB — the very bottom of the band — and 250 PB is unreachable without all-flash. This single answer moves the bid by a factor of two.
  2. Is "PB" decimal or binary? If PiB, every capacity number rises 12.6% and the 8-rack case fails.
  3. Is a FIPS-certified SED required at 26 TB, and will ORNL accept a lower drive capacity or filesystem-level encryption if no FIPS SED SKU exists at that capacity?
  4. Does "POSIX-compliant namespace" require a native POSIX client, or is POSIX semantics over NFSv4.1/RDMA acceptable?
  5. What is SPECTRE-1's compute fabric — Slingshot, Ultra Ethernet, or InfiniBand?
  6. Where does the second TDS live, and does it require a cleared facility?
  7. Who provides key management for the self-encrypting drives? §3.3 lists RSA, LDAP, DNS and license management as Company-provided but omits a KMIP key manager.
  8. Will ORNL define the namespace-aging methodology for acceptance before award?
  9. What is the floor loading limit for DC-1N? No value appears anywhere in the package.
  10. Do RDHX fans and controls count against the 275 kW cap?
  11. Is the UPS-protected feed sized for full load or orderly shutdown?
  12. Can the maintenance PoP start date (12/15/2026) be reconciled with §5.1's "begins upon successful completion of Acceptance"?
  13. How is NIST SP 800-207 Zero Trust expected to apply to the parallel file system data path?

App. A

The no's — and why

Every option we ruled out, with the reasoning recorded. If any of the assumptions below change — particularly a POSIX clarification or a budget increase — several of these decisions should be reopened.

A.1  Platforms we are not bidding

NOVAST DataReopen if Q4 answers favorably

Genuinely excellent at small files and QLC efficiency, and on the §4.1 distribution the technology would perform well. Two requirements land against it.

POSIX  §3.2.2 requires "a single POSIX-compliant namespace." VAST has no native POSIX client — access is NFS, SMB and S3. NFSv4.1 with RDMA and nconnect gets close, but for MPI-IO across a Slingshot or InfiniBand fabric, POSIX semantics over NFS is a substantive technical difference, not a marketing one.

Containers  §3.2.3 states a preference against container orchestrators in the data path and control plane. VAST's DASE architecture runs its CNodes as Docker containers. No Kubernetes is required, so it is arguable — but it means arguing against the grain of a stated preference while also arguing the POSIX point.

Decision: do not bid unless ORNL answers Q4 in favor of NFS-delivered POSIX semantics. Losing one of those two arguments is survivable; losing both is not.

NOPure Storage FlashBlade //S and //ECategory mismatch

FlashBlade is an excellent unstructured-data platform, but it is not an HPC parallel file system. Namespace scale at 150–250 PB, POSIX semantics, and per-endpoint parallel bandwidth at 120 × 400G are all outside what the architecture targets. //E is capacity-optimized for a different workload profile entirely — nearline and backup, not aged scratch under IOR and mdtest.

Decision: no. Bidding it would fail §3.2.2 and §4 on the technical evaluation regardless of price.

NODell PowerScale (OneFS)Category mismatch

OneFS is a scale-out NAS with NFS and SMB access, not a parallel file system with a native POSIX client. It cannot deliver parallel client bandwidth at this endpoint count, and the same §3.2.2 POSIX argument that blocks VAST applies here with less compensating performance.

Decision: no.

NOQumulo · NetApp · general object storageCategory mismatch

None of these are HPC scratch file systems. Object storage fails §3.2.2 outright — a bucket namespace is not a POSIX namespace, and no amount of gateway layering makes it one for MPI-IO under acceptance test conditions.

Decision: no.

NOBeeGFS (ThinkParQ)Support depth

Technically capable and used in parts of DoD HPC. The disqualifier is commercial, not technical: a six-year fixed-price PoP in a classified enclave, with a Q-cleared on-site analyst (§5.5), a two-week self-sufficient parts cache (§5.2), a 30-day CVSS v4 remediation SLA (§3.1.8), and the full 800-53 High artifact package (§3.1.13) demands a support organization of a scale BeeGFS does not have in the US federal market.

Decision: no.

NOHammerspace — as the primary namespacePartial: overlay only

Parallel NFSv4.2 and the Tier 0 concept are interesting, and Hammerspace could add value as a data-orchestration overlay. But as the primary 150–250 PB scratch namespace carrying acceptance risk under §4.2, the deployment track record at this scale and in this environment does not support it.

Decision: not as primary. Revisit as an overlay only if ORNL raises a specific data-movement requirement it solves.

A.2  Architectural options we ruled out

NOAll-HDD as the primary bidStill priced as an alternate

It reaches the capacity requirement — roughly 184 PB usable in the 8-rack envelope — and it is the cheapest path to raw petabytes. It fails on the other two axes simultaneously.

Power  87 JBODs at ~1,300 W consume 114 kW of a ~138 kW budget, leaving around 24 kW for the entire server layer. That funds roughly 20 × 400G endpoints against a permitted 120.

Small files  72.3% of files are ≤64 KiB. Serving that population from spinning media, at 70% aged utilization, under mdtest, against published specifications, is the worst possible combination of §4.1 and §4.2.

Decision: price it because §1 requires all three options, but do not lead with it. If a competitor leads with all-HDD on price, the power arithmetic in §03 is the argument that beats it.

NOAll-flash as the primary bidStill priced as an alternate

This one is a genuinely close call, and the reason for the "no" is not technical. All-flash fits the facility envelope better than anything else: ~250 PB usable within the power budget, using about half the available rack space, supporting 48–64 endpoints.

Cost  250 PB of QLC is on the order of 4,000 high-capacity drives. For a scratch file system this is very hard to justify against the alternative.

Schedule  §1 explicitly cites "unusual market conditions causing long-lead times." NAND allocation through 2026–27 is the specific risk the RFP is warning about, and delivery is Q1 CY2027.

Endurance  Scratch is write-heavy and churny. QLC DWPD against six years of scratch turnover needs modelling before anyone commits to it.

Decision: price it as the premium alternate and state the lead-time risk honestly. If ORNL answers Q1 such that 250 PB is required in 8 racks, this becomes the only compliant answer and we should be ready to pivot to it.

NOFlash + object capacity tier (WEKA-style tiering)Architectural

Tiering a flash namespace to an object capacity tier is how several vendors would close the capacity gap economically. It runs into §3.2.2's "single POSIX-compliant namespace" — a tiered architecture with an object backing store is defensible as one namespace but invites the argument — and it adds a second hardware footprint the 8-rack, 138 kW envelope has no room for.

Decision: no. If a vendor proposes it, the footprint and power must be counted in full, not treated as out-of-band.

NOMaximizing endpoint count to the 120 ceilingDesign choice

§3.2.2 permits up to 120 HSN endpoints and it is tempting to bid to the ceiling as a differentiator. At ~1.1–1.2 kW per 400G server, 120 endpoints alone consume roughly 130 kW — essentially the entire 8-rack budget, before a single drive.

Decision: no. Right-size endpoints to the power budget, show the arithmetic, and surface the capacity-versus-bandwidth trade explicitly rather than letting the evaluator discover it. A bid that silently under-delivers endpoints looks worse than one that explains why 120 is not physically reachable.
